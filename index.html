Objective

To determine the wavelength of sodium light with the help of a Newton's rings setup.

Apparatus

  • Plano-convex lens and plane glass plate arrangement
  • Sodium lamp
  • Travelling microscope with eyepiece
  • Plane glass plate inclined at 45°
  • Spherometer, plane glass plate, table lamp

Formulae

λ = (D²n+p − D²n) / (4 p R)
R = l² / (6h) + h/2

Dn = diameter of the nth dark ring · p = number of rings between the two readings · R = radius of curvature of the lens · l = distance between spherometer legs · h = sagitta (difference of spherometer readings).

Division of amplitude

When light falls on a thin film, the wave reflected from the upper surface interferes with the wave reflected from the lower surface. Reflection at a boundary with a rarer medium produces no phase change; reflection at a boundary with a denser medium introduces a phase change of 180° (π), i.e. an extra path difference of λ/2.

For an air film of thickness t viewed at near-normal incidence, the effective path difference is 2t − λ/2. Hence:

Bright: 2t = (2n − 1) λ/2   ·   Dark: 2t = n λ

In a plano-convex lens resting on a plane plate, the air film is circularly symmetric, so fringes of equal thickness become concentric circles. At the point of contact t = 0, giving a dark central spot.

Geometry of the air film

From the property of a circle, r²n = t(2R − t) ≈ 2Rt. Substituting the dark-ring condition 2t = nλ:

r²n = n λ R   ⟹   D²n = 4 n λ R

Therefore D²n is linear in n. A graph of D² vs n is a straight line through the origin whose slope is 4λR. Measuring the slope gives λ once R is known.

Ray diagram of the setup

Light from the sodium lamp is reflected down by the 45° plate onto the lens. The reflected rays from the top and bottom of the air film travel back up into the microscope, where they interfere.

Real-World Applications

Move your mouse over the 3D models below to explore how thin-film interference is used in modern engineering.

Anti-Reflective Coatings

Eyeglasses and camera lenses are coated with a precise thin film. The light reflecting off the top and bottom of the coating cancels out (destructive interference), eliminating glare.

Optical Flat Testing

Manufacturers place a perfectly flat glass plate over a lens. If Newton's rings appear, it reveals microscopic imperfections, allowing quality control down to nanometers.

Liquid Refractometry

By placing a liquid drop between the lens and plate, the ring diameters shrink. Chemists use this shift to precisely measure the refractive index of unknown liquids.

About this project

A project-based learning (PBL) submission for Physics Laboratory-1, Department of Physics and Materials Science and Engineering.

The aim was to go beyond a digitised lab manual by building an actual virtual bench: the wavelength is hidden inside the simulation, the student must align the crosswire, record readings, fit a graph and compute the result — exactly as on a real bench.

Learning outcomes

  • Understand division of amplitude and thin-film interference
  • Relate ring diameter to ring order and lens curvature
  • Use a travelling microscope and a spherometer
  • Fit a straight line and extract a physical constant
  • Estimate maximum probable error
